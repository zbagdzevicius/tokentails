# Creative Europe MEDIA — Video Games and Immersive Content Development 2027 — proposal draft

Built from the fact base. Frame: eu-cultural (a European game developer building an original narrative adventure). Sections follow the 2026 award grid (Relevance 35, Quality 25, Project management 20, Dissemination 20) and the mandatory work packages WP1 Artistic development, WP2 Technical development, WP3 Financing, distribution and marketing.

## Relevance: originality and creativity of the concept and story <!-- criterion: C1 | limit: 7500 -->

Pilka (working title) is a narrative exploration adventure for PC and mobile. The player is a street cat in a real European city, and the story is told the whole way through: in conversations with residents, in the places the cat remembers, and in the scrapbook of the volunteer who links the chapters. There is no score, no timer and no fail state. The player walks, listens, talks, remembers and chooses, and the story is what they carry from one city to the next.

The concept. Every street cat had a life before the street, and a person who left. The game is about why people leave animals behind, and it answers without cruelty: most of the people in it left out of loss, illness, eviction or a move for work. The player recovers each cat's past in the city where it happened and decides where the cat goes next. The result is a warm, funny story about European cities and the people in them, told from knee height.

The frame. Ona is a retired tram driver who volunteers at a Vilnius shelter and keeps a scrapbook of every cat that passes through. When a grey tabby called Pilka arrives, Ona starts writing to shelters in other cities about the cats she has sent on to new homes. Each chapter opens on a page she has written and closes on a page the player completes. In the last pages Ona, who has spent the game finding homes for others, has to decide whether to adopt a cat of her own.

The four cities. Vilnius (Užupis courtyards; Pilka and a student who moved abroad for work). Paris (a barge on the Seine; Mistigri and a bargewoman taken ashore by ambulance). Berlin (a Kreuzberg courtyard; Knopf and a musician evicted by rising rent). Barcelona (the rooftops of Gràcia; the kitten Tapa and a family about to move away). The story runs from Lithuania through France and Germany to Spain, and the thread between the chapters is people in different countries cooperating across borders on a problem they share.

Against existing work. Cat games are an established genre, and we place Pilka against the closest titles honestly. Stray puts the player in a cat's body in a fictional city it must escape; the cat has no past and the story is about the city. Little Kitty, Big City is an open-world comedy sandbox in which the cat's goal is to get home; it has no backstory and no human drama. The Case of the Golden Idol reconstructs events from frozen scenes as a deduction test that does not change the world the player moves through. What Remains of Edith Finch tells family memories through a different interaction per story, but the player only witnesses them. Pilka combines three things none of these do: a real European city per chapter, a cat's recovered past that changes the present-day city around it, and human stories about abandonment told without villains.

What is ours. The story, characters, script and art are new, made for this project and owned by the applicant. The narrative cast is designed by hand for this game and is separate from the applicant's live casual game, whose cat stories and portraits come from an AI pipeline [F-019]. None of that content, and none of its characters, is used in this game or in this dossier.

Real cases, where a shelter agrees. Our plan is to base each chapter on the real case of a rescued cat, supplied by a local shelter under a written agreement. No agreement is signed yet. The game does not depend on them: each chapter is written from the city's public shelter practice, and a signed agreement adds a closing page showing what happened to the real animal. We treat the Paris adoption café where we co-hosted a shelter-cat event [F-023] as a first contact, not a partnership.

## Relevance: level of innovation <!-- criterion: C2 | limit: 6500 -->

We claim innovation in three places the call names: gameplay, the visual approach and interactivity. Each one is built and shown in the prototype.

1. Gameplay: the past rewrites the present. In most narrative games the player's discoveries change what they know. In Pilka they change the city. Each recovered memory changes how residents treat the cat, which routes open through the district, and which homes the closing choice offers. Progress, story and level layout are one system. In Vilnius, once Pilka remembers waiting at a courtyard gate, the gate opens onto a new route to the river, the café owner Jonas starts leaving food by his door, and his café becomes a possible home.

2. Visual approach: the city repaints itself. The districts are hand-painted in layers. A district starts in the muted, rain-washed palette of a stray's day, and each recovered memory repaints the parts of the district it touches in the warm palette of the life the cat remembers: a lit window, a café awning, a wet street turning gold. The player can see, at a glance, how much of the cat's past they have recovered. The same layers carry the state of the world, so the art direction is also the progress system, and no interface counter is needed.

3. Interactivity: a different sense per city. The core rule stays the same, so the game is easy to learn, but each city changes the sense through which the past returns. In Vilnius the player reassembles torn photographs; in Paris they layer the river's sounds until the night on the barge plays back; in Berlin they play a musician's records in order and the flat rebuilds around them; in Barcelona they follow a family recipe across the rooftops, step by step, to the right kitchen door. Each medium grows out of its city, and each is designed to be read by both sound and image, so it is accessible by design rather than by adaptation.

Platform. The game is built from the start for both mouse and touch, in short scenes, so one save plays at a desk and on a commute. The engine is chosen in month one against written requirements: export to PC and both mobile stores, a layered pipeline for hand-painted art that supports the repainting effect, and external text tables for localisation. The decision record, with the test results, is part of the game design document.

## Relevance: sustainable and environmentally respectful development <!-- criterion: C3 | limit: 3500 -->

Our strategy covers how we work, what we ship and how we report it.

How we work. The team works remotely by default, from Vilnius and from the contractors' home cities. We travel only for the industry events and publisher meetings in the work plan, and we take the train for trips where a rail journey is practical. Contractors deliver digitally. We use development hardware for its full life and buy only the share the project needs, as the budget shows under equipment.

What we ship. Pilka is a digital-only game with no physical editions or merchandise in the development phase. The art is 2D and hand-painted, which runs well on older phones and low-power laptops, so players do not need new hardware to play. The prototype sets energy rules for production: a frame-rate cap, a battery-saver mode on mobile, and no always-online requirement. A game that runs well on the phone a player already owns is a game that does not push them to replace it.

How we report. We record the project's travel, and the hosting and build services it uses, and report them at the final review, with the actions we will carry into production. We follow the published guidance of the Playing for the Planet Alliance for games studios and report against it.

## Relevance: gender balance, inclusion, diversity and representativeness <!-- criterion: C4 | limit: 3500 -->

In the content. The central human character is a woman in her sixties, Ona, whose story carries the whole game. The supporting cast spans ages, classes and backgrounds: a young woman vet in Paris who doubts she is suited to her work, a Berlin kiosk owner, an evicted musician, a family forced to move, a café owner who cannot admit he is lonely. The game treats poverty, illness and eviction as the ordinary reasons people give up animals, and never as a moral failing. Each city is drawn from local reference, and each chapter's script is read by a native speaker from that city before it is locked, to catch stereotypes and errors.

In accessibility. The game has no fail state and no timed input, every sound clue has a visual equivalent and every visual clue a sound, colour is never the only way to tell things apart, and every interaction works with one finger on touch or one button on mouse. These are design rules, set out under Quality.

In how we manage the work. We recruit the contracted roles (narrative lead, artist, composer and sound designer) through open calls with written criteria and a shortlist balanced by gender. We publish the fee range in each call. We record the team's gender balance at the start and at the end of the project and report both. Playtests of the design are recruited to include women, older players and players with disabilities, and the recruitment criteria are written before the first session.

## Quality of content and activities <!-- criterion: C5 | limit: 15000 -->

Storytelling. The game tells one story across four cities through Ona's scrapbook, and four smaller stories, one per cat. The tone is warm and funny rather than sad: the cats are stubborn, vain and brave, and the humans around them are kind in clumsy ways.

Vilnius. Pilka, a grey tabby who rules the courtyards of Užupis, remembers a student who moved abroad for work. Supporting characters: Ona, and Jonas, a café owner who feeds strays but insists he does not like cats. Closing choice: stay with Jonas at the café, or go to a family Ona has found. Pilka's arc runs from pride to trust; Jonas learns to admit he wants a companion.

Paris. Mistigri, a black cat who grew up on a barge on the Seine, remembers the night her bargewoman was taken ashore by ambulance and did not come back. Supporting character: Lucie, a young shelter vet. Closing choice: the care home where the bargewoman now lives and cat visits are allowed, or Lucie's flat above the clinic. Mistigri's arc runs from fear of the shore to curiosity; Lucie finds her confidence by treating the cat no one else can handle.

Berlin. Knopf, an old tomcat in a Kreuzberg courtyard, remembers a musician packing his records while the rent letters piled up. Supporting character: Emre, a kiosk owner who bought the records and keeps them behind his counter. Closing choice: the musician's new, smaller flat across the city, or a quiet life at Emre's kiosk. Knopf's arc is about accepting a slower life; Emre decides to find the musician and give the records back.

Barcelona. Tapa, a kitten born on a rooftop in Gràcia, remembers only her mother and the smell of one family's kitchen. Supporting characters: the Serra family, who are about to move away, and their teenage son, who wants to leave the kitten behind. Closing choice: go with the Serras to their new home, or stay in Gràcia with a neighbour who knew Tapa's mother. Tapa's arc runs from recklessness to belonging; the Serra son learns that moving does not mean leaving everything behind.

Script excerpt, Vilnius, Jonas's café, first rain.

JONAS: Not you again. I do not feed cats. (He slides a saucer under the table.) That is for the pigeons.
PILKA (inner voice): The pigeons have never looked this handsome.
ONA (at the door, shaking out her umbrella): Jonas, you have fed "the pigeons" every morning since June.
JONAS: A café has to keep its customers.
PILKA (inner voice): I am not a customer. I am management.

Gameplay and its integration with the story. The player explores a hand-painted district from the cat's point of view, through streets, courtyards and rooftops. They talk to residents, follow what the cat notices, and find traces of its past. A trace opens a short memory scene, in which the past plays out once the player has put its fragments in place. What the memory shows then changes the present-day district: who talks to the cat, which routes open, which homes are possible. Every verb serves the story. Exploration is how the player meets the city's people; the memory scenes are how the story is told; the closing choice is where the player decides what the story means.

A memory scene from start to finish: "The Suitcase" (Vilnius).
- Traces. Pilka finds three torn pieces of one photograph: under the table outside Jonas's café, pinned to a courtyard noticeboard, and tucked into Ona's scrapbook. Each is earned by a story action: waiting out a rain shower under the café awning, knocking a flyer off the noticeboard, sitting on the scrapbook until Ona opens it and talks about the student.
- The scene. The player lays the pieces on a worn frame. Each carries a faint sound, a tram bell, a zip, a door closing, and when the pieces sit together the sounds play in order and the photograph comes to life. There is no wrong answer to punish: a mismatched arrangement shows a scene Pilka "does not believe", in her own words, and the player can try again.
- What it tells. The student who owned Pilka packs a suitcase in the courtyard and leaves a bowl by the gate.
- What changes. The courtyard gate opens onto a new route to the river, the district's palette warms around the gate, Jonas starts leaving food by his door, and his café becomes a home option in the closing choice.

Every memory scene is held to written rules: no fail state, no timed input, a trace that can be read by sound and by image, and a hint in Pilka's inner voice when the player hesitates, becoming more direct each time. A scene passes design review only when test players can say afterwards what the memory showed. A scene that is completed but not understood is rewritten, because in this game the memory is the story.

Level and character design. Each district is a small, dense map built around the places a cat's life happens: a café table, a courtyard gate, a noticeboard, a riverbank, a rooftop. It is laid out so that each recovered memory opens a route into a part of the district the player has already seen from outside. The WP1 level design document gives the Vilnius district map with its routes and gates, and the character sheets give Pilka, Ona and Jonas in their poses, expressions and palettes.

Visual approach. All art for this project is made by hand. A contracted artist paints the key art, the concept art, the character sheets and the district backgrounds in layers. The palette is warm and the linework soft, and the districts move from a muted stray's-eye palette to warm memory colours as the player recovers the past. Mood boards, sketches and hand-made key art samples are in the key art annex.

Graphic and sound design. The interface is kept to a minimum: the scrapbook is the menu, the save screen and the map, and the district's own colours show progress. Sound is a storytelling channel, not decoration. Each city has its own sonic palette, recorded or composed from local reference: tram bells and church bells in Vilnius, the river and the quays in Paris, a record collection in Berlin, a market and a kitchen in Barcelona. A contracted composer and sound designer writes the original score and designs every memory sound so that it also has a visual cue.

Accessibility. All dialogue is subtitled with speaker names, every sound clue has a visual equivalent, colour is never the only way to tell things apart, text size is adjustable, and every interaction works with a single finger on touch or a single button on mouse. An external accessibility and user-experience expert reviews the design document and the work-in-progress builds against these rules before the prototype is delivered.

Track record. The applicant has shipped a live casual game on the App Store and on Google Play [F-015] [F-016], with five game modes, including an 80-level platformer and a 30-level match-3 with leaderboards [F-018]. We cite it as evidence that we build, ship and maintain games on several platforms and pass store review on both mobile stores, not as a sample of this project's characters or art. Pilka is our first narrative game.

## Project management: development strategy <!-- criterion: C6 | limit: 9000 -->

Scope. The project covers the concept development phase only: from the story bible to the delivery of the first prototype, a vertical slice of the Vilnius chapter playable end to end on PC and mobile. Testing and debugging of that prototype, full localisation and release belong to production and are not part of this project or its budget. The prototype is delivered no earlier than ten months after the submission deadline, as the call requires.

Schedule. The project runs for eighteen months from the start date in the grant agreement. WP1 and WP2 run in parallel after month three; WP3 runs throughout.

WP1, Artistic development (months one to twelve). Story bible and treatment for the whole game; the full Vilnius script and outlines for Paris, Berlin and Barcelona; the game design document with the memory-scene rules; the Vilnius level design; the art direction guide with concept art and character sheets for Pilka, Ona and Jonas; the sound direction document. Deliverables: updated creative development (treatment, script, bible, game design document); declaration on independence and ownership; update on key crew. Review: sign-off by the narrative lead and the game director before production-quality art starts.

WP2, Technical development (months one to fifteen). Engine decision record in month one; the layered art pipeline with the repainting effect; the scrapbook interface; text tables for localisation; input for mouse and touch; the Vilnius vertical slice. Deliverables: video of and link to the prototype, and a teaser. Review: the prototype is delivered at the end of month fifteen.

WP3, Financing, distribution and marketing (months one to eighteen). Estimated production budget and financing plan; publisher shortlist; store page and wishlist campaign; pitch package localised into the languages of the chapter cities; publisher meetings with the prototype at European industry events; an interoperable standard identifier (ISAN or EIDR) for the work. Deliverables: updated financing, budget and production schedules; updated distribution and marketing strategies; the identifier.

Team. The game director and producer runs the project, owns the design, the budget, the contracts and the reporting, and chairs each review. The lead programmer owns the engine, the art pipeline and the builds; the gameplay programmer builds the district, the memory scenes and the input for mouse and touch; the game designer owns the game design document, the level design and the memory-scene rules. Four roles are contracted: the narrative lead (story bible and scripts), the artist (all key art, concept art and character sheets, by hand), the composer and sound designer, and the accessibility and user-experience reviewer. The team annex names each person with their credits, and person-months per role are set out in the budget table.

Partnerships. Shelter partners supply real cases where they agree in writing; the game does not depend on them. Publishers are approached in WP3 with the prototype. Contracted roles are selected through open calls before their work package starts, each with a second candidate kept as a replacement.

Management and risks. A fortnightly milestone review runs against the work plan, and each work package ends with a formal review of its deliverables.
- Scope: the prototype covers one chapter; later chapters stay at outline level.
- Engine: chosen in month one against written requirements and a test build, before art production starts.
- Contractor delay: each subcontract ties deliverable dates to the reviews, and a missed date triggers the replacement.
- Shelter partner lost or not signed: the chapter uses the city's public shelter practice, and gameplay is unchanged.
- Eligibility: the applicant's registered main activity is changed from publishing to game development before submission, and the sales report of the previous work is filed in the MEDIA Database dossier.

Eligibility, stated openly. The applicant is a small partnership registered in Lithuania on 2024-10-14 with NACE code 58.21 [F-021]. The call does not accept publishing companies, so before submission we register game development as the company's main activity. Our previous work is the live game on the App Store and Google Play [F-015] [F-016], and its sales report covers the period the call sets. The rights to the story, characters and script in this application are held by the applicant under dated and signed documents provided with the application.

Use of AI tools. Generative AI writing tools were used to draft and edit parts of this proposal's text. The team has checked every statement against its own records, and the tools used, the parts they were used for and the sources consulted are listed in the annex on AI use. No AI-generated art is used in the dossier or in the game.

## Project management: financing strategy and feasibility <!-- criterion: C7 | limit: 5000 -->

Development budget. The lump sum is built bottom-up in the budget table, by work package and category: staff for the four in-house roles, subcontracting for narrative, art, music and sound, the accessibility review and localisation of the pitch materials, travel to industry events and publisher meetings, and an equipment share. The grant covers the call's funding rate of the eligible costs, within the cap. Launch marketing after the development phase is excluded from eligible costs and paid by the company.

Co-financing. Our own share of the development budget is an equity contribution by the company's owners. It does not rely on sales revenue. A signed commitment and proof of funds are in the financing plan annex.

Production financing. In WP3 we cost production of the full game (four chapters, full localisation and release on PC and mobile) and build its financing plan. The plan combines a European publisher deal for marketing and distribution, the owners' continued contribution, and national and regional games funding where the company or a co-developer qualifies. The financing plan annex gives the estimated production budget and the target share from each source.

Potential to attract a publisher. We approach publishers only with a playable prototype, a costed production plan and a store page with a wishlist record. The shortlist is built in WP3 against written criteria: a European base, narrative or family titles released on both PC and mobile, and local marketing in the countries of our chapter cities. Letters of interest received during the project are added to the production financing plan.

Sales potential and revenue streams. Pilka is a premium game sold as a single purchase on PC (Steam) and on the App Store and Google Play, with no advertising, no in-game currency and no paid random rewards. Revenue comes from the base game in each release language, from later platform versions, and from licensing the story in other formats where a partner asks. The price is set with the publisher against comparable narrative games.

## Dissemination: European and international exploitation and distribution <!-- criterion: C8 | limit: 6000 -->

Transnational appeal. Every European city has stray and abandoned animals, so a player in one country recognises the story of a cat from another. The theme reaches players who do not usually buy narrative games, including families and animal lovers, and the writing and art are built to satisfy those who do.

Crossing borders: story and cast. Four chapters in four countries, with residents who speak with local names, food and street life, so the players who live there recognise their own streets. Ona's scrapbook ties the cities into one European story.

Crossing borders: localisation. Every text string, voice line and interface element is kept in external tables and separate from the art, so a new language needs no rework. The pitch package, the store page and the teaser subtitles are prepared in English and Lithuanian by the team and translated into French, German, Spanish, Italian and Polish during the project, which covers the language of every chapter city. The full game is localised into the same seven languages in production.

Crossing borders: team. The in-house team is based in Vilnius. The contracted roles are recruited through open European calls, and each chapter's script is read by a native speaker from its city.

Distribution. For release the game targets Steam on PC and the App Store and Google Play on mobile. We already publish on both mobile stores [F-015] [F-016], so our store accounts and review process are proven, and our Android app has 1K+ downloads [F-016]. We do not hold audience data by EU country, so we claim no existing European reach. We seek a European publisher for marketing and distribution in production, as set out under financing.

Indicators. We track, per EU country where store data allows: store-page wishlists, teaser views, press and creator coverage, publishers contacted, publisher meetings held and letters of interest received. Each indicator is reported at the final review with the publisher package.

## Dissemination: marketing strategy <!-- criterion: C9 | limit: 5000 -->

Unique selling points.
1. Play a street cat whose past changes the city around it.
2. Four real European cities, each with its own way of remembering: photographs, river sounds, records, a family recipe.
3. A hand-painted city that repaints itself as the player remembers.
4. A warm, funny story about why people leave animals behind, told without villains.
5. Playable by anyone: no fail state, no timer, one-finger or one-button play.

Target audiences and markets. First, players of cosy and narrative games on PC and mobile. Second, cat lovers and families who do not usually buy narrative games. The first markets are the chapter countries, Lithuania, France, Germany and Spain, and then Italy and Poland, whose languages are in the pitch package.

Reaching audiences early. The store page opens with the teaser in WP3 and every campaign is measured in wishlists. Each chapter city gets its own announcement in its own language. Where a shelter partner signs, it shares the teaser with its supporters, so a chapter is also a local campaign for real adoptions; the plan does not depend on it. We recruit design playtesters from our existing app community and from cosy-game player groups, which also builds the first wishlist base.

Promotional activities. The teaser is released with the store page. We present the prototype to publishers and press at European industry events in WP3, and we pitch the game to creators who cover cosy and narrative games, in their own languages. Each activity has a wishlist target set in the marketing plan and is reported against it at the final review.
