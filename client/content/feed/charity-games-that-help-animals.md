Games can help animals when someone attaches real money to playing: a sponsor pays per answer, ads fund a shelter, a purchase shares its price, or a stream raises donations. The good ones name the shelter, state the amount per action, and let you check that the money arrived.

This guide explains how each model works, gives real examples, and ends with a checklist you can use on any game, including ours.

<!-- key-takeaways -->
- In a free charity game, the money comes from someone else: a sponsor, advertisers, or the game company.
- Trust a game when it names the recipient, states the amount per action, and shows proof.

## How games can help animals

There are four common models. Each one has a different answer to the only question that matters: who pays?

- **Sponsor-funded actions.** A sponsor promises a donation per action, such as per quiz answer or per in-game feeding. It costs the player nothing; the sponsor's budget sets the limit.
- **Ad revenue.** The game or app shows ads and gives part of the ad income to a charity. The more people use it, the more ads are shown.
- **Purchases.** Part of the price of an item goes to a shelter. The share matters: "50% of each $5" and "all proceeds" are very different promises.
- **Streams and events.** Players or creators stream a game and ask viewers to give to a shelter directly. The game is the reason people gather, not the payment channel.

## Real examples

These are described from their own pages and from press coverage. We have not audited any of them.

**Freekibble.** A free trivia site. It says: "Click on an answer and we'll provide kibble and supplies to animal shelters". Right or wrong answers both count, and its sister site Freekibblekat does the same for cats. According to the site, the funds are paid by Greater Good Charities to the benefiting organizations as a grant. ([freekibble.com](https://freekibble.com/))

**Tab for Cats.** A browser extension from Gladly that turns each new tab into a small page with a few ads. Gladly says the ad revenue funds its charitable work, and Tab for Cats supports Greater Good Charities' The Jackson Galaxy Project, which supports shelter cats and the people who care for them. ([tab.gladly.io/cats](https://tab.gladly.io/cats/))

**Avia's Play for Paws (2025).** A time-limited event in the mobile games Solitaire Clash and Bingo Tour. Players could feed animals in the game; Avia said it would donate to the Nevada SPCA for every feeding action. The animals in the event were real rescue animals at that shelter. ([Business Wire](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20250818503896/en/Avia-and-Nevada-SPCA-Team-Up-to-Help-Sheltered-Animals))

**Stray (2022).** Playing Stray did not trigger a donation. Its launch inspired charity streams and fundraisers for cat shelters, and press reports described its publisher, Annapurna Interactive, supporting shelters such as the Nebraska Humane Society. ([80.lv](https://80.lv/articles/how-stray-has-made-a-real-life-impact-on-cat-rescue-efforts/))

## A checklist before you trust a charity game

Use these six questions on any game that says it helps animals.

1. **Is the recipient named?** "A real shelter in Kaunas" or "the Nevada SPCA" is checkable. "Animals in need" is not.
2. **Is the amount per action stated?** You should be able to tell what one quiz answer, one win or one purchase is worth to the shelter.
3. **Who pays?** A sponsor, advertisers, the game company or you. Each is fine, as long as the game says which.
4. **Can you check it without trusting the company?** Grant reports, receipts from the shelter, or public transactions all count. A big number on a banner does not.
5. **Is it still running?** Many campaigns last a few weeks. A page from last year may describe an event that has ended.
6. **Is the small print honest?** If only part of a purchase reaches the shelter, a trustworthy game says what part.
